Redesigning first-time use for new receivers

01Where it got hard

The platform had millions of receiver accounts, but only a small fraction ever transacted, and most stopped using the platform within three months. Receivers are asked by their customers to create a free account to get paid, so they arrive with money waiting, and the first-time experience couldn’t answer the only question they had: where is my money, and when does it land?

User quotes

Payment uncertainty created real emotional drag.

"When I meant cumbersome, there was like a lot of stuff that I knew I didn't need… I don't understand why all this stuff is here, I'm just trying to get paid."

Timing clarity mattered more than feature discovery.

"The biggest challenges are, timing, understanding when a payment is going to be made… It's nice to see a due date but that doesn't mean that that is when we will receive payment."

The payment process itself wasn't intuitive.

"I had gotten a notification on my phone that I have an invoice… I didn't know exactly what that meant."

How I found out. I went through the receiver experience myself, creating a payor account on our testing platform to pay a fictional receiver and mapping the journey. With a researcher, I ran a 10-participant study from signup to first payment (5 SMBs, 5 independent contractors) and a survey of recently signed-up receivers (1,056 responses). Customer experience and sales helped surface blockers, such as bank verification failures delaying payment.

Root cause #1

Receivers don't know what value the platform offers.

A majority of survey respondents said they signed up because a customer asked them to.

Root cause #2

No single source-of-truth for payment status, causing them to ask "Where is my money?"

The biggest sources of friction were uncertainty about payment timing, confusion about whether action was required, and a lack of clarity about what the platform was doing on the user's behalf.

Root cause #3

Receivers are uncertain about what actions to take after signing up.

Some receivers thought they were receiving a one-time payment and were done.

Problem Statement

How may we clarify payment status, offer best next steps and introduce product value to new receivers after they sign up?

02The decision I made, and why

I redesigned the “Get Started” page, where new receivers land after signing up, to be payment-status-first: answer “how and when will I get paid?” before teaching anything else. The product tour became optional and moved later. The redesign also moved the experience onto the design system.

Why: timing clarity beat feature discovery in every research signal, and invoicing, network and upsell messaging at the moment of maximum anxiety was noise. The journey map shows that moment.

The hard part was that “where is my money?” has a different answer in every state: whether a payment is on the way, whether an invoice exists for it, how the receiver gets paid (ACH or instant transfer), and whether bank verification succeeded. I mapped those variables into a decision tree so every state got a clear answer and a next step.

03What I tried and dropped

  • Video tutorials. Dropped for launch because of production cost.
  • A mandatory walkthrough. Made optional: seven of ten participants thought it made sense, but not all wanted to go through it.
  • Early invoicing, network and upsell messaging. Pulled back from the moment of peak anxiety. “Network” only made sense to people when it was tied to their actual payment.
  • The Concept A layout. Six of ten preferred Concept B as more logically ordered and easier to learn from, so I adopted it.
  • Vague language. Rewritten: “Advanced” sounded expensive or complex, and “arrival date” didn’t say when money would actually hit the bank.

04What changed

Measured head-to-head against the legacy first-time-use flow: +30% invoices created, +22% initial TPV (Total Payment Volume), +21% incremental TPV. Charts are for illustration purposes only.

The strongest takeaway for me was that comprehension changed behavior: once receivers could more clearly see how and when they would get paid, more of them created invoices, completed the flow, and moved more money through the platform.

It compounded, too. Improving first-time understanding became a growth lever: the invoicing flow later became a major paid-tier entry point and contributed meaningfully to paid-tier acquisition and receivable TPV. Trust and clarity at first use keep paying.

05Supplier onboarding vision

The research and learnings of this project served as the foundation for an agentic vendor onboarding vision 3 years later.

The data pointed out only 39% of vendors who opened onboarding finished it, and the password step alone lost about 20% of vendors a month. Leadership wanted an "onboarding agent," but nobody had defined what it should actually do.

I was the lead designer for Phase 1. My job was to turn "build an agent" into a concrete interaction model that vendors would trust with their tax ID and bank details.

I did two things.

First, I defined a principle that the agent should deliver value before asking for commitment. The payment shows up first, and the password moves to the end. Our Maze study with 24 vendors showed their unwillingness to commit to account creation before seeing their first payment. One vendor said, "I just want to get paid, not have to manage another account."

Second, I designed the trust model for the agent's work. The agent pre-fills the business profile from the invoice, AP data, and directory data, so the vendor confirms a profile instead of filling out a form. Every pre-filled field stays editable. The mental model I gave users is "the agent drafts, you confirm."

I cut the path from 10+ steps down to 3 before first payment. The team set targets of 39% to 50% completion and 75% to 80% at the password step.
