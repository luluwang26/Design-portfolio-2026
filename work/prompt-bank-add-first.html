Prompt bank add first during set up

A guided path to the one action that unlocks value.

At a glance
Summary:
Research said users who add a bank first convert more, but onboarding made adding a funding method look no more important than nine other tiles. The product manager had the hypothesis; my job was to design the UI that delivered it. The answer was a “Start here” section pinned to the top of the Get Started page, guiding high-intent users straight from verify email to add bank.
Approach:
Data-led experiment design, translate a research-backed hypothesis into a concrete UI, then validate it as an A/B test. Control: 50% sees no guided flow entry point. Variant: 50% sees the guided flow entry point. Nothing else is changed on the page.
Scope:
The set-up guide for new trialers, right after account creation and before seeing the product.
Team:
1 product manager, 2 product designers, 5 engineers, 1 analyst, 1 marketing partner.
5+% Lift in banks added
10+% Lift in organizations converted
10+% Lift in ICP organizations activated

01Context and problem

To use either the Payables or Receivables workflow, a user must first add a bank account. About a third of users already read adding a bank as the crucial first step, yet only a small minority of organizations successfully completed it.

The trigger for this work was a failed adjacent experiment. A prior sync-first experiment lifted sync rate but dropped activation, conversion, and the rate at which people added a funding method. Users who synced immediately tried to pay the bills and vendors they had imported, only to be blocked by needing a bank and a verified email: a convoluted path to a first payment.

Legacy research pointed to the fix: prompting users to add their bank first leads to higher conversion. The problem lies in the information hierarchy: the set-up guide showed ten tiles with equal visual importance, new trialers are left to “choose your own adventure”, no guidance on the critical step “adding a bank” to unlock product values.

Problems with a “flat” set-up guide

Against “Hick's Law”

Faced with 10 equally weighted tiles, users had no path.

Lost conversion

The bank unlocks both bill pay and card acceptance, the moment most correlated with conversion and retention.

Set-up order matters

The sync experiment proved that prompting users syncing their software actively suppresses bank-add rate.

03Competitive analysis

I looked at some B2B software guided set up designs and here's what I recommended to the team:

  • Zoom: 2 steps. The 1st step contains prerequisite actions, the 2nd allows fine tuning.
  • Stripe: Prioritize prerequisite steps (e.g., bank add) in set-up sequence.
  • MailChimp: Progressive disclosure. Start from the basics, gradually move towards advanced set-up.
  • Shopify: Group relevant steps together, give a birds-eye view while allowing detailed views.

04First round of concepts

I drafted 2 concepts to discuss with the team and received feedback. Since none of the directions are strong, I started another round of exploration.

05Second round of concepts

06Final concept and decisions

Elevate one action above all others. The core decision was to break the ten-equal-tiles pattern with a single prioritized section. The tradeoff: deliberately de-emphasizing other setup actions for the majority, in exchange for a clearer path to value.

Pair bank add with email verification. Sequencing verify email → add bank inside one guided section pre-empted the exact blocker the sync experiment had exposed: users hitting an unverified email at payment time.

Guide, don't gate. “Start here” sits on top of the page rather than replacing it, so confident users can still self-navigate. That preserved the experienced-user path while rescuing the lost majority.

07Experiment set up

The product manager owned the hypothesis: ask users to add a bank before they sync and create a bill, and I owned the UI that would achieve it, on a fast turnaround from hypothesis to design concept.

The solution slotted into the existing sync-experiment framework, replacing the sync tile with an add-a-bank-first treatment, so it could ship and be measured quickly.

The experiment rolled out 50-50 to all net-new trialers who landed on Set-up guide. Engineering flagged that the treatment needed bank-verification workflow to be seamlessly added.

08Outcome & impact

The “Start here” experiment won, and was my biggest design win that quarter. Banks added, organizations converted, and ICP organizations activated all rose together, carrying a seven-figure projected annualized revenue impact.

The result validated the central thesis: the failure wasn't user motivation or a missing feature — it was that the most valuable action was buried among equals. A single, well-placed guided section moved banks added, conversion, activation and revenue together, because each downstream metric depends on the bank being added first.

However, we discovered a downstream metrics impacted negatively: 60 day retention dropped for the variant.

09Reflection & next steps

  1. The value of reading a failed experiment correctly. The sync-first loss wasn't “users don't want to sync”, but a “one-size-fit-all” solution that failed to recognize different user group needs.
  2. Study user intent and create meaningful personas provides personalized guidance.
  3. Look at experiment win/loss through the lens of the whole funnel. Since the retention loss, we rolled back the treatment and started another round of experiment.
