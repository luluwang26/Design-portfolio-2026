Trust in “Who am I paying?”

AI-assisted vendor identity matching

At a glance
Summary:
Accounts Payable (AP) users struggle to trust the entities shown in network search, forcing manual verification and delayed first payment. We interviewed 8 users and found 3 ways to improve trust in new vendor add experience.
Design process:
Framing the question → 5 moderated test → 3 design prototypes → Synthesis and iteration → 3 experiments launched for directional hypothesis validation.
Team:
1 product manager, 1 product designer, 1 front-end engineer, 1 back-end engineer.
3+ experiments to validate initial hypothesis
50%+ targeted search success rate lift

01Context & Problem

The users are Accounts Payable (AP) specialists, bookkeepers, and finance staff who pay vendors on a recurring basis. When AP users add a new vendor, they struggle to trust the entities shown in network search, forcing manual verification and delayed first payment. This leads to low search success rate, which is defined as the share of searches where the person paying bills (the AP clerk) picks a vendor from the first page of results. What must the vendor card communicate and which signals earn trust?

02Research & Learnings

The unmoderated Maze test surfaced clear, consistent behaviors that defined the design space.

For the test, we gave participants a task: “You need to pay your vendor, Boston Tea, $800.00 by Feb 28th, locate, add, and confirm the correct vendor.” What we measured: Selection confidence (opinion scale), comprehension of the terminologies such as “connections” and “virtual card”, and open-ended expectations and decision criteria.

Learning #1

They actively distrust vendor listings, and have learned to compensate.

When asked whether they ever have difficulty finding the correct vendor to pay, four of five said yes, citing concrete reasons: duplicate listings with different email addresses, accounts created by staff who have since left, and information that's simply outdated or doesn't match what their contact provided.

Learning #2

They treat connection count as a trust heuristic.

When asked what else they'd want to know before deciding, a participant revealed the mental model: “The number of connections. So often we have multiple choices to select and try to go with the one with the most connections if there are multiples.”

Learning #3

They want freshness, not just accuracy.

Several users described a verification ritual, confirming by email that the account is still active and that the vendor still prefers ePayments. One user wanted a way to “prompt [the vendor] to update their information if we know something is out of date.”

Learning #4

Multi-modal entry points to vendor add flow

Often times APs will drop an invoice or W-9 of the vendor instead of manually typing vendor info to add a vendor.

Learning #5

Vendor policy in place

Larger AP teams have vendor identity policy in place that requires W-9 collection before payment.

Here's a list of data points our users prefer to see in order to validate a new vendor.

  • Account last updated data.
  • Account created date.
  • # of connections/customers.
  • Vendor preferred payment method.
  • A way to verify vendor by first and last name (esp. contractors).
  • A way to verify vendor by searching by Tax ID.

03Constraints

The card had to operate inside the existing add-vendor flow, where a user searches, the business address auto-fills, and they proceed toward paying a bill.

The card also had to work with the data the network actually has — connection counts, payment method (virtual card), business name and address — and represent each in plain language for non-technical finance users.

04Design Concepts

The first round added all useful vendor data points for APs to review. Key decisions made:

  • Showing the data proven by users to be useful for verification.
  • Adding an AI feedback question to improve once live.
  • Adding a confidence score icon on the card itself for quick judgment.

For the second round, I included document drop entry point experience. Key decisions made:

  • Adding “document drop” as the main entry point. In the future, the system can monitor and pull directly from Inbox for new invoices.
  • Don't guess with money. Giving user the power to make final call on which vendor to select.

For the 3rd round, instead of asking user to open a drawer to verify all data points, I make them appear directly in the chat area.

05Experiments Launched & Next Steps

Unfortunately, this project was cut short before we could launch an A/B test with the legacy vendor add flow. We ended up launching 3 incremental experiments on the legacy experience to validate the direction.

  1. Payment details. Hypothesis: We believe showing payment details on the cards of VC vendors will raise search success rate, because users can confirm it's the right vendor without leaving the results. Test: cards with payment details vs. without.
  2. Specific trust signals vs. a generic badge. Hypothesis: We believe specific signals, such as last active date and number of connections, will raise search success rate more than a generic “Verified vendor” badge. They show users why a vendor can be trusted, not just that it can. Test: “Verified vendor” badge vs. specific signal tags.
  3. Confidence score with a reason. Hypothesis: We believe a confidence score on the vendor card, with a short reason why it's likely the right vendor, will make users more confident in their pick and raise search success rate. Test: cards with the score and reason vs. without.

After the 3rd round of iteration, I referred back to our interview notes on vendor validation workflow, and confirmed with users that 1. To avoid paying to the wrong bank, they will ask the vendor to provide payment method info, if it is not present on the invoice already. 2. If the vendor info recently changed, they will update their existing record for this vendor. Based on this feedback, I plan to add an optional step for APs to reach out to vendors for bank info, as well as updating the last step to show the full and most updated vendor data, reduce anxiety on what has changed.
